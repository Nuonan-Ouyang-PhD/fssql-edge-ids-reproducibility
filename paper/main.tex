\begin{abstract}
Edge intrusion detection requires detector selection to account for classification quality, processing deadlines, temperature, and energy. Feasible-Set Shielded Q-learning (FSSQL-R) combines a device-calibrated latency/thermal guard with a masked tabular policy. We evaluate its implementation on Raspberry Pi 3B+ and Pi 4B 8GB platforms, separating calibration, online deployment, and detector-generalization evidence. Independent held-out joint guard coverage is 2879/2880 on Pi 3B+ and 2877/2880 on Pi 4B. The Pi 3B+ guard excludes one detector under a binding workload, but FSSQL-R underperforms Safe-Greedy in F1 and reward. The Pi 4B evaluation comprises 60 online runs, 36,000 windows, and 576,000 record evaluations, with direct DC input-energy measurement. FSSQL-R and Safe-Greedy have similar mean F1 without background load and under moderate background load (approximately 0.960), whereas under the highest tested load their mean F1 is 0.8869 and 0.9608, respectively. A post hoc trace analysis links 1322/3000 high-load FSSQL-R windows to unvisited training states, zero-valued Q rows, and the fixed tie-breaking action. Neither the learning layer nor the measured energy results establish a consistent advantage over simpler controllers. The findings distinguish calibrated action admissibility from end-to-end safety and show why state support, false positives, controller cost, and physical energy must accompany average detection scores in evaluations of learning-based intrusion detection system (IDS) schedulers.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Lightweight edge devices increasingly execute intrusion detection close to data sources, but deployment is constrained by latency, thermal headroom, and energy. A detector that performs well offline may be unsuitable for a particular device or workload. Inference cost differs substantially across models and devices \cite{banbury2021mlperftiny,reddi2019mlperfinference}, and thermal state further constrains on-device execution \cite{tan2024thermalnpu}. Keeping several detectors available and selecting among them online therefore offers an alternative to always executing a single model. The scheduler itself, however, consumes time and introduces another source of error.

Reinforcement learning (RL) can optimize sequences of decisions, but unconstrained exploration may exceed operating limits. Approaches based on constrained Markov decision processes (MDPs) typically limit expected costs \cite{altman1999cmdp,achiam2017cpo,chow2018lyapunov}. Runtime shielding instead removes actions that fail an admissibility test before the policy acts \cite{alshiekh2018shielding,carr2023partialshielding,koenighofer2025shields}. Safety-aware RL has also been applied to intrusion detection system (IDS) remediation in 5G-enabled IoT \cite{malwi2026safetyaware}. These approaches motivate separating a learned objective from a device-specific feasibility check; they do not imply that learning will outperform a simpler controller.

We study Feasible-Set Shielded Q-learning (FSSQL-R) for choosing among four resident IDS models under 40-ms modeled-latency and \SI{82}{\celsius} operating caps. We address four research questions (RQs). RQ1 asks whether the calibrated guard attains its held-out coverage and restricts model choice when resources are constrained. RQ2 asks whether Q-learning adds value beyond a same-guard greedy rule and a no-training threshold controller. RQ3 asks what controller-time and physical-energy costs are incurred in online execution. RQ4 asks how the frozen detectors perform on the official UNSW-NB15 test set. Experiments use a Raspberry Pi 3B+ and an independently calibrated Raspberry Pi 4B, which provides a second hardware configuration.

The evidence does not support a general advantage for Q-learning. Safe-Greedy performs better in the Pi 3B+ experiment, in which the guard is binding, i.e., it excludes one of the four detectors. In the Pi 4B experiment, the learned and greedy policies have similar detection scores under lower loads, but the learned policy degrades under the highest background load. Direct energy measurements also fail to show consistent savings. The contribution is a measured account of these boundaries:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt]
\item A device-specific feasible-action formulation with separately fitted predictors, residual margins, and held-out coverage, together with an explicitly conditional safety statement.
\item Matched-seed comparisons against greedy, fixed-model, and threshold controllers, including false-positive rate (FPR), per-seed outcomes, and a trace-level analysis of unsupported Q states.
\item Online timing on two Pi configurations, and direct DC input-energy measurements across three Pi 4B background-load settings, with explicit accounting boundaries.
\item Separate reporting of scheduler-development workload performance and the saved fixed-threshold evaluation of four detectors on all 82,332 official UNSW-NB15 test rows.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work and Positioning}
\subsection{Sustainable scheduling and adaptive edge inference}
Recent work in sustainable computing increasingly treats resource management as an online control problem rather than a static configuration task. Wang \emph{et al.} use deep reinforcement learning (DRL) for runtime GPU frequency capping \cite{wang2024drlcap}; Ko \emph{et al.} schedule energy-harvesting IoT sleep states while preserving reconstruction quality \cite{ko2025rass}; Gao \emph{et al.} jointly optimize latency and energy for in-network task scheduling \cite{gao2025inc}; and Li \emph{et al.} learn dynamic voltage and frequency scaling (DVFS) policies for multitask edge workloads under deadlines \cite{li2025dvfsedge}. Related work also addresses adaptation in IoT anomaly detection under concept drift \cite{xu2024conceptdrift}. These studies motivate dynamic resource-aware control, but their actions are frequency, sleep, task-placement, or adaptation decisions rather than selection among heterogeneous on-device IDS models.

Closely related edge-inference work reinforces the need to account for workload variation and the cost of the controller itself. Tan \emph{et al.} formulate workload-adaptive edge inference request scheduling as an MDP and use RL to select among model-serving choices \cite{tan2024workloadadaptive}; Zhang \emph{et al.} co-optimize DVFS and edge-cloud offloading for latency/energy efficiency \cite{zhang2024dvfo}; and thermal-aware scheduling has been studied explicitly for mobile DNN execution \cite{tan2024thermalnpu}. Our problem differs in two respects: model selection is constrained by a separately calibrated per-window feasible set, and our evaluation measures instrumented online controller latency and physical input energy on the target device rather than relying only on a modeled cost.

\subsection{Lightweight and online intrusion detection}
Lightweight IDS research spans classical one-class methods, extreme learning machines, compact deep models, and TinyML-style deployment \cite{tax2004svdd,huang2006elm,doriguzzi2020lucid,fusco2024tinyids,warden2019tinyml,lin2020mcunet}. Recent security literature has emphasized adaptation as well as compact execution. Nakip and Gelenbe propose an online self-supervised IDS that adapts without conventional offline relabeling \cite{nakip2024selfsupervised}, while Yang \emph{et al.} address lightweight dynamic open-set intrusion detection for the industrial IoT (IIoT) \cite{yang2025openset}. These works focus on improving or updating the detector. The practical problem here is complementary: the detectors are frozen before formal evaluation, and the runtime controller decides which detector to execute under latency, thermal, and resource constraints.

\subsection{Safe RL and shielding}
Safe RL covers expectation-constrained optimization, Lyapunov methods, and action shielding \cite{garcia2015safesurvey,achiam2017cpo,chow2018lyapunov,alshiekh2018shielding}. The novelty of FSSQL-R does not lie in shielding itself; rather, it instantiates a calibrated, device-specific feasible-action layer for multi-model IDS scheduling and separates this layer from utility optimization. Malwi \emph{et al.} \cite{malwi2026safetyaware} use a runtime safety shield for self-healing remediation actions in 5G-IoT. Our action semantics and constraints are different: the actions are heterogeneous IDS models, and admissibility is derived from device-specific latency and thermal measurements under explicit operating caps.

\subsection{Positioning against simpler controllers}
Learning should be compared with controllers that do not require training. The baseline set therefore includes Static-Light, Round-Robin, Safe-Greedy, Unshielded-Q, and a conventional threshold controller. Safe-Greedy uses the same calibrated feasible set and one-step reward as FSSQL-R, choosing the action with the largest immediate reward. Unshielded-Q uses the same state, reward, and Q-learning implementation without feasible-set masking. The threshold controller uses only measured state and fixed development-time thresholds. The numeric Q-learning configuration used here is reported explicitly, fixed before formal evaluation, and evaluated against these simpler alternatives. \section{Problem Formulation}
Decision windows have duration $\Delta=100$~ms. Action $a_t\in\Aset$ selects one of four resident detector implementations: FISVDD, LUCID, TinyDL, and OI-SVDD+AS-ELM.
% TODO(author): expand the OI-SVDD and AS-ELM acronyms and add one or two sentences (or a small table) describing each detector: architecture, parameter count, and input features.
The state is
\begin{equation}
s_t=(z_t,T_t,b_t,q_t,a_{t-1}),
\label{eq:state}
\end{equation}
where $z_t$ is estimated traffic risk, $T_t$ is measured CPU temperature, $b_t$ is a controller-internal energy-state proxy, $q_t$ is queued backlog, and $a_{t-1}$ is the previous action. The energy state is not a measured battery state. In the Pi 4B online experiment $q_t=0$ throughout; no queue-recovery claim is made.

The label-free one-step reward is
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
r_t={}&z_t\,\mathrm{TPR}_{\rm val}(a_t)-(1-z_t)\,\mathrm{FPR}_{\rm val}(a_t)\\
&-0.1e(a_t)-0.05\min(q_t/256,1)\\
&-0.02\ind[a_t\ne a_{t-1}],
\end{split}\label{eq:reward}
\end{equation}
with no switching penalty on the first action. $\mathrm{TPR}_{\rm val}$ and $\mathrm{FPR}_{\rm val}$ are the true- and false-positive rates of each detector on validation data and remain fixed. The term $e(a)$ is a normalized compute/energy proxy, not measured joules. Its Pi 4B values are 0.352513, 1.000000, 0.426251, and 0.584404 in the action order above and are unchanged across B0--B2. The energy state starts at 1 and decreases by $0.001e(a_t)$ per window.
% TODO(author): state how b_t is reset or clipped during the 6000-window Q training (otherwise it would fall below 0).
Ground-truth evaluation labels do not enter this reward.

Let $U_\tau(s,a)=\widehat\tau(s,a)+\epsilon_{\tau,a}+\delta_\tau$ and $U_T(s,a)=\widehat T_{t+1}(s,a)+\epsilon_T+\delta_T$ be upper predictions with one-sided residual margins and fixed buffers. The admissible set is
\begin{equation}
\Asafe(s)=\{a:U_\tau(s,a)\le40\;\mathrm{ms},\ U_T(s,a)\le82^\circ\mathrm C\}.
\label{eq:safeset}
\end{equation}
The guarded quantity is the calibrated action/service measurement, not an unconditional bound on every operation in the runtime. When the set is empty, the implementation flags fallback and selects the fixed FISVDD action; that fallback is not guaranteed safe. No accepted online run used it.

\section{Feasible-Set Shielded Q-learning}
\subsection{Device-specific calibration}
Latency prediction is an empirical fit-split 0.99-quantile lookup indexed by action, batch size, and background setting. Temperature uses a standardized one-step linear model with eight design columns. Separate fit and margin windows determine the predictor and positive-residual 0.99 quantiles. Fixed buffers of \SI{10}{ms} and \SI{0.6}{\celsius} are added. These quantities are frozen before held-out coverage is evaluated; the supplement specifies residual distributions and device-specific margins.

\subsection{Masked updates and evaluation}
For nonterminal transitions the update is
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
Q(s_t,a_t)\leftarrow Q(s_t,a_t)+\alpha_t\big[&r_t+\gamma\max_{a'\in\Asafe(s_{t+1})}Q(s_{t+1},a')\\
&-Q(s_t,a_t)\big].
\end{split}\label{eq:q}
\end{equation}
Four bins each for risk, temperature, energy, and backlog, and five previous-action values, produce 1280 states and 5120 state-action pairs. The bins, learning schedules, and replay settings are in Table~\ref{tab:params}. The Pi 4B policy is trained for 6000 windows per seed using 16-record batches (denoted n16), the Pi 4B thermal transition model, and development data without background load (B0; see Table~\ref{tab:params}). Formal B1/B2 evaluation does not retrain it. Q values start at zero. Evaluation uses $\epsilon=0$ with no Q or replay updates; a tie selects the lowest admissible action index. This also defines behavior in previously unvisited states.

\subsection{Conditional admissibility}
\begin{proposition}
If the one-step positive latency and temperature prediction errors do not exceed their respective residual margins plus fixed buffers, an admitted action satisfies the modeled caps in \eqref{eq:safeset}.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For an admitted action, $U_\tau\le\SI{40}{ms}$ and $U_T\le\SI{82}{\celsius}$. Under the stated error bounds, the corresponding realized quantities do not exceed these upper predictions.
\end{proof}
Empirical quantiles do not establish these bounds for all future states. Coverage, observed cap exceedances, and online timing are therefore reported separately. Zero observed violations are not a proof of zero risk.

\section{Experimental Method}
\subsection{Implementation provenance and test chronology}
The detectors, preprocessing, calibration, and scheduler are implemented for this study and frozen before formal evaluation. The Pi 3B+ online configuration uses a documented stabilized power and cooling arrangement.

An internal implementation audit found that the worker used in an initial Pi 4B online campaign did not consistently implement the declared batch size, risk/controller logic, reward, and background-load behavior, and that its timing and energy alignment were affected. That campaign is excluded. All Pi 4B numbers reported here come from the final campaign, which uses the 16-record inference path, configuration-driven thresholds, masked selection, and contemporaneous load-process records. The excluded attempts are archived but are not pooled with the final campaign.

UNSW-NB15 official training data \cite{moustafa2015unsw} provide group-separated model-training, validation, and scheduler-development partitions. Validation TPR/FPR alone supplies detector-quality terms to the reward; official test-set statistics do not enter the reward, the detector thresholds, or any scheduler setting.

The official detector test was evaluated once, on 11 September 2026. The final scheduler implementation and the Pi 4B online runs were completed afterwards using frozen development inputs, without reopening the test. The four detectors, preprocessor, thresholds, and validation-quality file retain the hashes recorded at the test evaluation. Thus the saved test results concern those fixed detectors; the later scheduler evaluation is not an untouched-test evaluation. The preserved test aggregates allow confusion-matrix recomputation, but do not provide a complete prediction lineage for independently regenerating all ranking metrics.

\subsection{Hardware and guard evidence}
The Pi 3B+ uses a heatsink, continuously running fan, and open-top case; the Pi 4B 8GB uses active cooling. The governor is fixed to \texttt{performance}. Hardware faults and departures from the specified starting conditions invalidate an attempt, independently of its scientific outcome. Each platform has separate fit, margin, and coverage splits of 2880 windows: four actions, six batch sizes, three background settings, and 40 observations per cell. Predictor parameters and margins are not transferred between devices.

\subsection{Controllers and workloads}
Safe-Greedy maximizes the complete one-step reward in \eqref{eq:reward} over the same admissible set as FSSQL-R, including security utility, the normalized energy cost, backlog, and switching penalties. It is a same-guard, same-reward myopic baseline; FSSQL-R instead selects the admissible action with the largest learned Q value. Unshielded-Q, evaluated on Pi 3B+, removes the masking from the same Q implementation. Round-Robin cycles through all models. Static-Light always selects the predesignated light action, FISVDD, despite its poor detection performance.

The Pi 4B Threshold controller is a no-training, unshielded baseline with hysteresis. It selects FISVDD when $T\ge\SI{80.1495}{\celsius}$ or $n+q\ge512$; it selects TinyDL when $T\le\SI{78.1495}{\celsius}$, $n+q\le256$, and $z\ge0.7$; otherwise it retains the previous action, initially FISVDD. These are configured thresholds, not values inferred from test outcomes. The temperature/queue branches are implemented but are not activated in the evaluated cold, zero-backlog regime; their implementation is checked separately.

The Pi 3B+ binding comparison contains six policies, ten matched seeds, and 600 windows/run. Pi 4B compares FSSQL-R, Safe-Greedy, Threshold, and Static-Light at n16/B0, n16/B1, and n16/B2, using seeds 5101--5105. For each seed a permutation of the 6000 development records defines $i_{w,j}=\pi[(16w+j)\bmod6000]$. The same 600-window schedule is used across policies and loads. This gives 9600 record evaluations per run and 576,000 across the accepted matrix; because records are reused, these are not independent observations. Risk $z_t$ is the mean of the 16 per-record outputs of a frozen risk-proxy model. Labels are stored separately and loaded only after the decision loop for scoring.

B1/B2 start a separate worker pinned to CPU 3 at configured 0.5/1.0 duty in a 100-ms period. PID, affinity, duty, and process liveness are recorded during each run. No independent process-utilization time series is available, so these values describe the programmed load rather than a measured CPU percentage. Run order is fixed within seed blocks; cooldown uses the documented idle-temperature criterion. The final campaign retains 58 originally valid runs and two replacement runs, re-executed because the initial temperature checks of the original attempts failed. Both failed attempts remain archived, and their metrics are never mixed with those of the replacements.

\subsection{Timing and physical energy}
Models remain resident. Pi monotonic timestamps measure preprocessing, risk estimation, telemetry, state construction, latency/thermal guards, admissible-set construction, policy selection, action binding, and inference. ``Controller'' runs from after preprocessing through action binding. Q lookup and policy selection are separately timed within this controller interval. End-to-end (E2E) time runs from preprocessing start through post-action telemetry; reward calculation, row serialization, and end-of-run label scoring are outside that E2E boundary. Switching timing measures resident action binding, not model reload. Fixed 100-ms scheduling targets are retained, including jitter and overruns.

A KM003C USB-C power meter is sampled by a separate host computer, outside the Pi decision path. Power samples are integrated over their actual timestamps by the trapezoidal rule between the host's receipt of the Pi's start and end acknowledgments (ACKs), with at least 5~s of surrounding samples and no endpoint extrapolation. This is absolute DC input energy without idle subtraction, not AC wall energy. The acknowledged interval includes scheduled idle periods, background load, and end-of-run scoring/writeout; its observed duration is 60.070--60.282~s. $E/600$ is run energy normalized per window, not a resolved 100-ms energy trace. No measured joules are substituted into the frozen proxy reward.

\subsection{Statistical analysis}
The unit is the matched run/seed, not a window or repeated record. We report all seeds and use means/SDs for continuous summaries and median/range for FPR. Paired differences use a two-sided exact sign-flip test and 2000 paired percentile-bootstrap resamples (seed 20261022). At five pairs the smallest two-sided sign-flip $p$ is 0.0625; intervals and tests therefore need not yield identical decisions. Multiple endpoints and policy contrasts are descriptive, with unadjusted $p$ values reported in the artifact, not a family of confirmatory superiority claims. All five Pi 4B seeds are retained. State-support diagnostics are explicitly post hoc and do not trigger retraining.

\section{Results}
\subsection{RQ1: Guard coverage and action admissibility}
Table~\ref{tab:coverage} retains every held-out miss: one on Pi 3B+ and three thermal-bound misses on Pi 4B. Exceeding a thermal prediction bound is not the same as exceeding the \SI{82}{\celsius} operating cap. The Pi 3B+ binding experiment has three admissible actions throughout, excluding LUCID; the Pi 4B experiment has four throughout. Hardware and workload both differ, so this contrast is not a controlled estimate of processor-generation effects.

Pi 4B records no observed latency/thermal cap violation or fallback. Its maximum E2E latency is 18.375~ms, maximum recorded temperature is \SI{47.712}{\celsius}, and minimum recorded CPU frequency is 1800~MHz. These conditions do not establish the effectiveness of binding thermal control. The Pi 3B+ Threshold run contains one retained 52.522-ms E2E event, dominated by a 50.414-ms controller interval while inference takes 0.446~ms. For that action, the point prediction is 5.147~ms and the guarded upper prediction is 15.703~ms, leaving 24.297~ms to the cap. The cause of the controller tail is not resolved by these timings; the observation limits an unconditional complete-pipeline safety claim.

\subsection{Training support and the high-load result}
A post hoc lookup of each runtime state in the frozen Pi 4B training visits identifies 0/3000 unvisited-state windows in B0, 1/3000 in B1, and 1322/3000 in B2. Every one of the 1322 B2 windows has an all-zero Q row and selects action 0, FISVDD, under the fixed lowest-index tie rule. This identifies a reproducible execution path; it does not estimate a failure probability. Temperature, energy-state bins, and previous-action histories determine which states are reached; no controlled ablation separates their causal contributions here.

The Pi 4B training checkpoints give mean rolling reward 0.4736 at 600 windows and 0.5541 at 6000, with 22.2/73.4 and 25.8/91.0 visited states/state-action pairs, respectively. The final mean greedy-policy change rate is zero over the checkpoint comparison, but mean absolute temporal-difference (TD) error remains 0.1293 and most states are unvisited. Pi 3B+ has similarly sparse support (34.9/118.9 at 6000 windows). These are finite-budget stability diagnostics on visited states, not evidence of full-state convergence. A separately planned Pi 3B+ queue-transition extension failed its accumulation/recovery preflight and produced no policy results.

\subsection{RQ3: Controller and physical-energy costs}
Across B0--B2, mean controller time is 4.673~ms for FSSQL-R and 4.516~ms for Safe-Greedy, compared with selected-detector inference of 0.520 and 0.441~ms. E2E means are 8.762 and 8.507~ms under the stated boundary. Controller work is thus material, but the timer excludes reward/serialization/scoring and should not be interpreted as all software overhead. The mean FSSQL-R switch count rises from 22.6/23.0 in B0/B1 to 162.8 in B2; Safe-Greedy has no switches. The proxy switching penalty and measured action-binding time are different quantities.

Fig.~\ref{fig:energy} shows absolute run energy for each policy and load. The paired FSSQL-R-minus-Safe-Greedy energy differences are $+0.908$~J (B0), $-1.209$~J (B1), and $+0.645$~J (B2). The direction is not consistent across loads; exact paired $p$ values are 0.125, 0.125, and 0.5625. FSSQL-R mean DC input power rises from 2.948~W to 3.325~W and 3.733~W across the three configured loads. These measurements describe operation of the Pi, controller, resident detectors, and load worker over the acknowledged interval, not energy attributable to an individual inference. They do not support a general energy-saving claim.

\section{Discussion}
\subsection{Admissibility is not detection quality}
The Pi 3B+ results establish that a calibrated guard can restrict the action set; the Pi 4B results show a nonbinding regime under the same nominal cap. Neither observation proves that shielding is universally necessary. In particular, a feasible FISVDD action can have unacceptable FPR. A resource shield constrains modeled latency and temperature, not classification quality. That distinction matters when the learned policy reaches an unvisited state and its tie-breaking rule selects a resource-feasible but weak detector.

\subsection{Does the learning layer earn its cost?}
Neither evaluated Pi regime establishes a practical need for the learned layer over the simpler same-guard rule. B0/B1 Pi 4B detection scores are close, while B2 and the Pi 3B+ binding comparison favor Safe-Greedy. FSSQL-R also incurs more switching. The policies share the same one-step reward and guard, so the comparison evaluates learned Q selection against immediate reward maximization. Its interpretation remains limited by the model-based training environment and sparse state support. In addition, the zero-backlog experiments do not test the queue-control part of the formulation. The appropriate conclusion is limited to these policies and workloads, not that RL can never help IDS scheduling.

The trace-supported B2 failure path suggests an evaluation requirement: report the fraction of online states supported by training, the action taken on an all-zero Q row, and that action's detection quality. Mean performance alone can hide these decisions. No after-test policy change is made to suppress the unfavorable B2 result. A support-aware default or a wider training distribution would be a separate future study, not a retrospective correction to this experiment.

\subsection{Sustainability and measurement boundaries}
Recent sustainable edge-control studies act directly on GPU caps, sleep states, task placement, or DVFS \cite{wang2024drlcap,ko2025rass,gao2025inc,li2025dvfsedge}. Our action is detector selection under a fixed governor, and a normalized resource penalty is not an electrical energy measurement. Direct DC input measurement therefore tests rather than assumes energy savings. Here, background-load changes dominate the policy energy differences, and no consistent FSSQL-R advantage is established.

Adaptive edge inference and online IDS studies \cite{tan2024workloadadaptive,zhang2024dvfo,nakip2024selfsupervised,yang2025openset} also motivate examining the complete deployed decision path. Our measured controller intervals exceed detector inference, and the Pi 3B+ outlier occurs despite substantial action-level guard headroom. Timing scopes, model residency, end-of-run work, and meter boundaries must therefore be stated explicitly.

\section{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
The empirical scope is two actively cooled Pi-class configurations and UNSW-NB15; microcontroller (MCU) applicability is prospective. There is no deployment near the \SI{82}{\celsius} cap and no accepted queue accumulation/recovery experiment. Joint empirical coverage is not a distribution-free guarantee outside the sampled hardware conditions. The two devices are not a single controlled cross-device benchmark: their workload, calibration, and training histories differ.

Pi 4B online inputs are reused scheduler-development records. The 576,000 record evaluations are not independent test samples, and five matched seeds per condition limit inference. Bootstrap intervals and unadjusted exact tests are descriptive, while the support analysis is post hoc. The load-process evidence establishes execution and configuration but not a separately measured CPU-utilization trajectory. ACK-bounded input energy includes tail work and is not inference-only energy. The public repository and submission source package provide derived summaries; complete raw-evidence recomputation requires the separate evidence archive.

The detectors and scheduler are implementations developed for this study, and their behavior may differ from other implementations of the same methods. The invalid initial Pi 4B campaign is excluded, with the corrections and replacement-cell selection documented in the supplement. The official detector test predates the final scheduler implementation: it was not reopened, but the scheduler evaluation is not a fresh blinded test study. Saved confusion counts are recomputable; ranking metrics have more limited retained provenance. These limitations constrain the claims and are not resolved by the release checksums.

\section{Conclusion}
FSSQL-R combines calibrated feasible-action filtering with masked tabular Q-learning for multi-model IDS scheduling. Independent guard coverage and online execution provide evidence for device-specific admissibility, not unconditional end-to-end safety. With direct DC input-energy measurement on the Pi 4B, the evaluation does not establish an incremental advantage for Q-learning: lower-load performance is close to Safe-Greedy, while high-load performance deteriorates through a documented unvisited-state/default-action path. Together with the Pi 3B+ comparison, these results favor testing simpler controllers before accepting the cost of learning, and reporting state support, false positives, and measured energy alongside average F1.
